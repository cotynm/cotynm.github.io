UX/UI

Financial management app design

Design of a desktop app for internal use by project directors.

Role
UX/UI designer
USA
2024
Users
Project directors

The project

Context

At the company, directors managed certain aspects of their projects through an Excel spreadsheet, which they kept expanding as they needed more features. Although they were used to it, it had become so complex that some procedures were tedious, forced or slow. On top of that, onboarding new people into the role had become very difficult.

Goals

UX

Give project directors a better experience managing their projects, refining processes and building an efficient, fit-for-purpose tool.

UI

Develop a desktop app so project directors can view, analyze and forecast their projects' billing and financial balances.

Challenges

  • There was no prior documentation of flows, procedures, processes, etc. All we had was an extremely complex Excel spreadsheet and the users' own experience.
  • The product would require integrations with other management apps: human resources, billing, estimates.
  • Processes weren't standardized: not all users followed the same procedures for each task, so criteria had to be unified.

Features and functionality

What do users need to get done?

  • Check billing status.
  • View totals.
  • Review and compare monthly and four-month closings.
  • Enter and review progress.
  • Send reports to clients.
  • Evaluate results by stage, month and four-month period.
  • Edit figures to make forecasts and projections.
  • Understand the impact of costs per period, etc.

The process

Wireframes

We had to build low-fidelity wireframes to test the different screens and proposed flows with users.

During the presentations we also kept gathering new feature requests and suggestions.

We also identified some pain points, both in processes and in data handling procedures, to consider in later stages.

One of the critical issues was that users wanted to edit every value at will. Although the spreadsheet allowed it, we decided to restrict it based on agreed criteria. This way, the tool shows real values, processes stay clean, and balances are clear and transparent for both the client and the team.

Architecture, flows and requirements

As we started designing the components and final frames, the project managers did intensive work to validate, standardize and unify processes together with users.

Meanwhile, the development team began mapping integration needs with other platforms and planning development.

Visual design

Since there was no design system, I used the company's brand book as a starting point. While complete and versatile, it doesn't include visual elements or styles meant specifically for digital products, so I had to define new typefaces, color palettes and components.

Typography and color palette

The color and type definitions ensure compliance with accessibility standards for contrast, sizes, spacing, etc.

Color system and components

Icon system

As a tool for handling data and organizing it into tables and statuses, it required a wide color palette and a varied icon library.

For the typeface, I prioritized a font that holds up better for reading large amounts of data in long tables.

MVP screens

What did I learn?

This project came with a fair amount of complexity, constraints and challenges, for me as a UX/UI designer, but also for the team and for the company, which is just starting to bring a human-centered design approach into its internal tools as added value for its services.

  • Sometimes it's better not to rush the designWhen processes aren't clear, getting things right takes time and some back and forth.
  • The importance of documenting the processIt helps the team as much as it helps me.
  • Pivoting isn't wasted timeWhen it's about improving the product or its flows.
  • Data over white spaceMaking peace with the fact that most people prefer to see data or information rather than white space or visual breathing room.
  • Simplicity and consistencyDisplaying large amounts of data on a platform is a beautiful challenge that calls, more than ever, for simplicity and consistent flows and components.
  • Always design for more than one screen sizeEven when responsive design isn't required.
