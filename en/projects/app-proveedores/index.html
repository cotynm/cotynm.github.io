Vendor management app redesign

Rethought flows and UI design for an internal vendor management app.

Role
UX/UI designer
Platform
Microsoft Power Apps
USA
2024

The project

Context

The company's development team had built a solution to manage vendors (people or companies hired externally for specific jobs) using Microsoft Power Apps, which makes it possible to build apps quickly.

As users made requests, permissions, features and sections were added without strategic planning. Over time the app proved very useful and support requests went down, but there was still friction in the processes and, above all, a very steep learning curve for new users.

Goals

UX

Understand how users use the app to identify needed adjustments to processes, flows and features.

UI

Redesign the app to improve usability and the user experience. Bring the brand identity into the design.

Challenges

  • The constraints of the tool (Power Apps), since customizing templates and components has its limits.
  • The complexity of the integrations needed to import data from other platforms.
  • The technical team's availability: client work takes priority over internal projects.

Research

Both the surveys and the interviews were done with the area's staff and surfaced many findings, patterns and insights. Some called for rethinking processes and refining flows, and others showed a need to reinforce good practices and encourage them through the new design. Most of the input confirmed the hypotheses outlined when defining the problem, such as the need to work on usability and simplify some steps.

Planning the next stages with a prioritization matrix showed that most needs were high priority and didn't demand much effort from the team.

Still, since there were quite a few items, we decided to implement them in stages.

On the other hand, the different integrations would demand more from the development team.

At this stage we also set the task assignments for each area.

Wireframes and flows

As user flows were reworked, we built low-fidelity prototypes so we could validate quickly with the team.

When designing the user flows, we removed unnecessary steps and added shortcuts, confirmation messages, the ability to undo, and other actions that give users a greater sense of control and safety.

Project detail screen: previous app / low-fidelity prototype of the new design.

Visual design

For the visual design, on the one hand, we had no design system, so I used the company's brand book as a starting point.

On the other hand, the screen structure, size and layout, as well as the icons and table styles, were constrained by the customization options Power Apps offers. Following the development team's suggestion, we agreed to stick mostly to the platform's default styles and components, so as not to hurt the app's performance.

Typography and color palette

The color and type definitions ensured compliance with accessibility standards for contrast, sizes, spacing, etc.

Although I used the corporate palette as a base, I defined a new main color to set this app apart from the company's other internal tools.

For the components, I used the Power Apps material design kit available on Figma Community as a guide. It's limited, but it worked as a foundation.

What did I learn?

Being able to do research with users was very positive: it let us validate assumptions and move forward on firmer ground.

Timelines ran longer than planned, because we had to rethink some processes whose friction became clear as the project moved forward, and because of other priorities. Still, I believe we ultimately met the expected deadlines to address the needs that came up along the way.

  • Involving usersWhen you involve users in the research and tell them the goal, it's amazing how willing they are to help, feeling part of the improvement process.
  • A tool's limitations don't mean the final design can't be greatConfirming, once again, that product design is a whole, where architecture, navigation flows, UX writing, visual design and more work in balance.
  • Not everyone is used to reviewing flows through low-fidelity prototypesWhen presenting progress to people who haven't been part of day-to-day decisions, it's worth taking the time to build high-fidelity designs that come as close as possible to the final product.
