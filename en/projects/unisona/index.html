End-to-end UX case: app for single-parent families

End-to-end design process focused on the needs of heads of single-parent households. Final project of the Diploma in Human-Centered Design.

Role
Researcher, UX/UI designer, identity design
ARG
2022

The project

Personal context

In 2022 we were going back to a “normal” life after 2 years of pandemic, and I was coordinating a team at an inbound marketing agency.

Since I started my career in graphic design, I've always kept exploring other disciplines and specialties. As a teacher, back in 2010 I was already bringing UX and accessibility concepts into my classes.

Wanting to formalize and structure everything I had learned on my own, I took on the Diploma in Human-Centered Design at UNSAM.

They were intense months of classes and loads of new information, but thanks to an outstanding, generous teaching staff, top-notch guests and a great team, we developed this project from start to finish.

Research

Choosing the topic and research stage

The topic we chose, single-parent families, turned out to be as interesting as it was unfamiliar, since none of us live in a single-parent situation.

On the other hand, both the benchmark and the desk research turned up very little information on the subject, few digital solutions and little official or specialized information.

Findings: benchmark and desk research

Listening to people: surveys and in-depth interviews

We designed, ran and analyzed surveys. We scripted interviews and met with different profiles of potential users. Based on the data gathered, we prioritized ideas across 5 areas:

  • Demographics and general information
  • Financial situation
  • Outside help
  • Leisure and recreation
  • Relationships and social life

Empathy map, user persona and user journey

These insights helped us define our users, their needs, feelings and problems.

We built an empathy map and defined user persona profiles with their user journeys.

Ideation and design

Ideating the solution

Based on the research results and the user persona, empathy map, user journey, topic and affinity mappings, we started to develop our hypotheses and insights and frame a direction as a “how might we” (HMW).

HMW

“How might we help mothers raising single-parent families make parenting decisions?”

MVP

To start shaping the proposal, through brainstorming and an effort-impact matrix we arrived at two possible directions:

1.Help people organize their tasks through an app that lets them keep track of them and prioritize them.

2.Help them connect with other mothers in the same situation, to support each other or, if nearby, to socialize.

With our mentor's help, we chose the second option. We believed its impact would be greater, since we hadn't found any product specifically addressing the need for connection, mutual support and socializing among peers.

With that direction set, we started thinking about our solution's features using a MoSCoW prioritization matrix.

User flows and wireframes

As user flows were reworked, we built low-fidelity prototypes so we could validate quickly with the team.

When designing the user flows, we removed unnecessary steps and added shortcuts, confirmation messages, the ability to undo, and other actions that give users a greater sense of control and safety.

Testing and adjustments

Testing with users

We set up the test to learn whether people could create an account, find a community and join it.

Starting from hypotheses about difficulties or doubts that might come up in the process, we could learn, for example: whether searches were intuitive, whether the information provided was enough to make decisions, and which elements or intermediate steps should be added or removed to help complete a task.

We defined 3 tasks and evaluated each one with these metrics:

  • Time
  • Success and error
  • Task difficulty
  • In-depth questions

And we organized the results in a matrix scoring the severity of each finding:

What did we find?

The test revealed two main friction points in navigating the prototype:

1.People chose to search from the search bar on the home screen rather than the “Search” option in the navigation. We decided to prioritize one of the two alternatives to avoid confusion.

2.The older participant, who has vision problems, struggled to tell “sign up” from “log in” apart, a sign that the product wasn't adapted enough. To fix it we proposed changes, like renaming “sign up” to “create your account”.

Final adjustments

Besides the changes that came out of testing, we reviewed the app and kept applying the tools and techniques we had learned:

  • We shortened the flows to make them simpler.
  • We defined basic accessibility guidelines through a test.
  • We checked that the app met usability heuristics.
  • We applied UX writing to refine the copy, calls to action and all the text on screen.

Bonus: brand development

Before user testing, we took some time to come up with our app's identity.

Naming: through brainstorming we grouped the ideas into 3 directions: community, single parenthood and solution.

After refining them we chose “Unísona”, a semi-invented word referring to a passage of music where all voices sing the same note. Besides reflecting one of the app's goals, it was short, original, memorable and easy to pronounce.

Logo: we created a logotype with a playful twist in its letters, which we also used to build a standalone symbol that works as an avatar. This lets the brand adapt to different needs and contexts.

For extra clarity, we added a tagline.

What did I learn?

Although I already knew how important Human-Centered Design is, and knew most of the techniques and tools involved, going through this process taught me a lot, raised many questions and sparked thoughts that still stay with me:

  • Richness, diversity and pluralityThe value of different perspectives and profiles at every stage of the process.
  • Paying close attention to avoid biasEspecially in the early stages.
  • Flexibility toward change and iterationAnd the importance of spotting friction early so errors don't carry over.
  • An outside perspectiveIn this case, our mentor, who pushed us out of our “comfort zone” and challenged our decisions constructively.
  • Rigor in assigning tasks and datesWith enough foresight to adapt in time.
  • A topic I'm passionate aboutAnd finally, confirming that this is a topic I'm passionate about and want to keep exploring.
